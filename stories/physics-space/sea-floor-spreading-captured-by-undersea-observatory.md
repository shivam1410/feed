A sea-floor monitoring experiment achieved the first in situ measurement of oceanic crust being created between tectonic plates at a mid-ocean ridge. Undersea observatories recorded the physical process of seafloor spreading directly. This contrasts with previous understanding derived from indirect methods like paleomagnetic measurements of old seafloor or seismic imaging. Direct observation provides unprecedented detail about spreading rates, crustal composition changes, and volcanic activity patterns. Such sustained seafloor monitoring opens new possibilities for understanding plate dynamics, earthquake processes, and ocean chemistry.
